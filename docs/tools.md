# The tools of the Value-to-Market Framework

This reference describes every tool the skill can run, grouped the way the method works: read the field, lay the foundations, understand the customer, design the proposition, set the company strategy, take it to market, keep it current, check it against reality, and package it.

Each tool runs inside a job (see the [command map](how-it-works.md#the-command-map)) or on its own with `/vp tools <name>`. For each one you will find what it answers, when to reach for it, what to give it, what you get back, how it reasons, and which book of the *Value by Design* collection goes deeper.

## The three books behind the tools

The tools apply the Value-to-Market (V2M) Framework that Hoang Huynh lays out across the *Value by Design* collection:

- ***Value Proposition Design*** covers the what and the who: the fundamentals of value, the Value Proposition Formula, the target and the anti-customer, offer mechanics, and the premise, promise and proof that keep a proposition honest.
- ***Value Proposition Strategy*** covers the why and the where: reading the field, designing a category, building a moat, and moving value through a market that resists it.
- ***Value Proposition Engineering*** covers the how, at scale: KPIs and validation, automation, AI integration, and the go-to-market machinery that makes value repeatable.

Each tool below names the book where its thinking is developed. The skill runs the procedure; the book explains why it works, when it fails, and how to judge the result.

## The ideas every tool shares

- **The Formula.** VP = [ Channels x Core Values x Promise ] + USP. The bracket multiplies, so one factor close to zero sinks the whole proposition; the USP adds on top.
- **The order of work.** Assets, core values, premise, promise, proof, category, channels. Tools run in this order inside a job.
- **The 3P diagnostic.** No premise and you are irrelevant; no promise and you are weak; no proof and you are not trusted.
- **Evidence tags.** Every fact is marked evidenced (with its source), inferred, or assumed.
- **Scorecards.** Each tool scores its result against a short sanity test and proposes another pass when the score is too low.

## Read the field

### source-read
**What it answers:** what does this website or document actually promise, to whom, with what proof?
**Use it when** you want an honest reading of your own site or a rival's, before any judgement.
**Give it** a URL, a PDF, a deck or pasted copy.
**You get** the value proposition as the source states it, every claim listed apart from every proof, the audiences addressed and the ones left out, pricing logic, calls to action and tone.
**How it reasons:** it writes down what the material says before evaluating it, so the later diagnosis can measure the distance between what a company says and what it is.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### benchmark
**What it answers:** who are we really up against, and where is the open water?
**Use it when** you need the competitive picture, or before choosing a position.
**Give it** your competitors, an industry name, or let it search.
**You get** players in four rings (direct competitors, other offers for the same job, alternatives including doing nothing, and analogous industries), a profile of each, the ocean you swim in (red, blue or new), a map of the field across five value lenses, your standing by lens, and the white spaces an asset of yours can prove.
**How it reasons:** it reads the field with the customer's yardstick and keeps only the white space you can back with something you own; an empty cell nobody can prove is a trap with a view.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### category-scan
**What it answers:** what does this category already expect, and what could we borrow from its neighbours?
**Use it when** you want to separate the table stakes from the places where the game is actually played.
**Give it** your category or industry.
**You get** expected values for the category and four rings around it (adjacent, similar, overlapping, loosely related), each classed as hygiene, differentiator or fading, and the moves other categories have that yours has not imported yet.
**How it reasons:** values wear out with use; what differentiated yesterday becomes hygiene today, so the scan also flags values that are fading into a standard.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### vp-audit
**What it answers:** how good is this value proposition, and what is its costliest gap?
**Use it when** you need a diagnosis before rebuilding, or a health check.
**Give it** the proposition (a site, a pitch, or your own words).
**You get** the fifteen-challenge audit scored green, yellow or red, the 3P headline, each claim placed on the Promise Ladder, the anti-patterns found, the wastes of value, the reddest structural gap with its cost, and the quickest fix.
**How it reasons:** it starts from your off-the-cuff version of the proposition and measures how far the properly written one ends up; unanswered challenges count as decisions the market is taking for you.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

## Lay the foundations

### business-sketch
**What it answers:** how does the business work, how will it evolve, and which triggers are already in motion?
**Use it when** you start a design or a strategy, or when you start from scratch.
**Give it** what you sell, to whom, how money comes in; or, from scratch, a space and an ambition.
**You get** a working model of the business, its lines, its evolution path over three horizons, the triggers already under way with the part of the proposition each will hit, and a first business model canvas. From scratch, it proposes two or three distinct directions and recommends one.
**How it reasons:** a proposition designed on a business nobody has described rests on guesses; the triggers already in motion decide how long any promise can hold.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### assets
**What it answers:** what do we truly own, and how hard is it to copy?
**Use it** before choosing values or claiming a position.
**Give it** what you own and control; it adds what the industry and the category hold.
**You get** an inventory across eight families (physical, technological, relational, human, reputational, community, institutional, narrative), each scored on exclusivity and on how hard it is to replicate, imitate or substitute, and the "crimes": defensible assets you are not monetizing.
**How it reasons:** an asset everyone in the industry shares can never be exclusive; only what you own can carry a defensible position. A positioning without assets is a pose.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design* and *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### core-values
**What it answers:** which values will we invest in, drawn from what we own?
**Use it** after the asset inventory, before any promise.
**Give it** the assets, your audience, and any value you must or must not stand for.
**You get** a value set (three to five primaries, secondaries, at least one antithesis you deliberately reject), each tied to the asset it draws on, and an evolution plan over three horizons with the triggers that would swap a value.
**How it reasons:** a value drawn from an asset you own is cheap to defend, because the asset already proves it.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

## Understand the customer

### jobs
**What it answers:** what progress do customers hire us to make?
**Use it when** you design for a line of business or test fit.
**Give it** the audiences and the situations that matter.
**You get** jobs at three levels (functional, social, emotional), across audiences and cases, stress-tested under pressure (stress, little time, few resources, little knowledge), tagged by objective, clustered into maps you rate as core, important or irrelevant.
**How it reasons:** a job is progress in a circumstance; the same product is hired for different jobs by different people, and a job under stress is often a different job.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### archetypes
**What it answers:** which customer and partner types do those jobs reveal?
**Use it when** the job set is large or several actors matter (buyers, users, partners, suppliers).
**Give it** the job set.
**You get** coded archetypes ("A, the precise lawyer", with variations A1, A2) placed on several axis pairs, with charts.
**How it reasons:** an archetype is defined by the jobs it hires you for, which keeps the targeting about progress instead of demographics.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### personas
**What it answers:** who do we target first, and who do we turn away?
**Use it when** you need faces for targeting and messaging, and a clear anti-customer.
**Give it** the archetypes.
**You get** personas marked by adoption maturity and buying role, and the antithetical personas: the customers who cost more than they yield, to repel by design.
**How it reasons:** naming who you refuse is as strategic as naming who you serve; it feeds the premise directly.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### canvas
**What it answers:** do we fit what customers care about most, and which features can go?
**Use it** before building or redesigning an offer.
**Give it** the jobs and your current offer.
**You get** the value proposition canvas (jobs, pains and gains ranked; products, pain relievers and gain creators), the fit read, a ranked feature set and a cut list.
**How it reasons:** fit only counts for pains and gains the customer ranks high; a feature that relieves nothing ranked is a candidate to cut.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design* (the canvas is by Osterwalder and Pigneur).

### adoption
**What it answers:** will people adopt it, and how do we test that cheaply?
**Use it when** "they don't get it", "nobody buys", or before a launch.
**Give it** the personas.
**You get** a Motivation, Opportunity and Ability grid per persona, the bottleneck, fixes specific to the mechanism, a map of the many who are not buying yet, and a validation plan with a test, a KPI, a threshold and a falsifier for each gap.
**How it reasons:** all three uprights or nothing; more volume only cures volume problems.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering* (MOA is by MacInnis, Moorman and Jaworski).

## Design the proposition

### 3p
**What it answers:** who is it for, what do we promise, and how do we prove it?
**Use it** at the heart of any design.
**Give it** the values, the personas and the antithetical personas.
**You get** the premise in belief form ("for all those who believe X, and not for the others"), with the exclusions and the risks they avoid; the primary promise and its sub-promises, each ingredient with an example and a design suggestion; the proof plan across four families with at least one proof that costs you something; and the open items list pairing each promise element with its proof.
**How it reasons:** premise, promise and proof are one contract; designing them apart loses the thread. A promise the proof cannot back gets resized.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### ladder
**What it answers:** how high does our promise reach, and is the climb earned?
**Use it when** the message feels flat, or before raising ambitions.
**Give it** the promise and the proof.
**You get** the current rung (Empty, Solution, Value, Cultural Resonance, Higher Call), the proposition written at each rung it can honestly reach with when and to whom to use each, guidance on when a Higher Call helps and when it backfires, and the climb plan with its effect on the P&L.
**How it reasons:** the ladder measures height, not truth; a high rung without the rungs beneath is an air-inflated promise.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### pricing
**What it answers:** what should we charge, and what are we leaving on the table?
**Use it when** setting or changing a price, or when margins feel wrong.
**Give it** prices, costs, any evidence of willingness to pay.
**You get** a benchmark of comparable prices, the Value Stick (willingness to pay, price, cost, willingness to sell) with its three slices (customer delight, margin, supplier surplus), moves that widen the stick before touching price, and pricing scenarios with their risks. The price decision stays yours.
**How it reasons:** compare value, never price alone; widen the stick first, then decide how to spend the delight.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design* (the Value Stick is by Felix Oberholzer-Gee).

### channels
**What it answers:** where do we say it, and where does the message contradict itself?
**Use it when** the message fragments across channels, or before a launch.
**Give it** your channels and touchpoints.
**You get** a triplet map (value, recipient, channel as one unit), spillover and contradictions, unconventional channels rivals ignore, a repair list ordered by damage, and a brief per channel.
**How it reasons:** a proposition that holds in one channel and breaks in another is perceived at its weakest point.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### formula
**What it answers:** does the whole proposition add up, or is one factor close to zero?
**Use it** at the end of a design, before polishing anything.
**Give it** the results of the design tools.
**You get** the filled Formula for one line of business with a verdict on each factor.
**How it reasons:** the bracket multiplies; polishing a strong factor while another sits near zero changes nothing.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### verbalization
**What it answers:** how do we say it, to each audience?
**Use it when** the design is settled and needs words.
**Give it** the design.
**You get** the one-sentence value proposition, payoffs for each earned rung, the word territories to own and to avoid, seeds for tone of voice, brand personality, mission, vision and purpose, a cleanup of all copy into one voice, and an elevator pitch per audience.
**How it reasons:** words carry only the value the design established; nothing is said at a rung the proof does not earn.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

## Set the company strategy

### category-design
**What it answers:** which category can we frame, name and claim?
**Use it when** competing inside the current frame keeps producing increments.
**Give it** your assets, the benchmark and the categories customers file you under.
**You get** the attributes customers value across direct, adjacent and similar categories, a composed new category, two framed candidates (a winner and a runner-up) with frame, name and claim, the ocean read with how fast the open water is filling, and the likely evolution of the category.
**How it reasons:** better is not different; leaps come from a new frame of reference, anchored in assets only you can prove.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### value-matrix
**What it answers:** how does value flow between us, customers, partners and suppliers?
**Use it when** you depend on an ecosystem, a platform or partners.
**Give it** the actors you work with.
**You get** an actor-by-actor matrix with the value and jobs in each live exchange, the type of each exchange, the backward flows a pipeline view hides, the intersections that deserve their own canvas, and the business model block each feeds.
**How it reasons:** value circulates through an ecosystem; marking only the cells that carry weight points the next investment.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy* (the Value Matrix is by Simone Cicero).

### defense
**What it answers:** what protects us: moat, bridges, leverage?
**Use it when** "can this be copied?", or before investing in a position.
**Give it** the assets and the likely attackers.
**You get** the ten-times-capital test (what a rival with ten times your money would still fail to copy), the moat types and their attacks, the killer features that build bridges to suppliers and customers, the levers to nudge, convince or force, and the moves that tilt the balance.
**How it reasons:** a moat without bridges isolates you, leverage without a moat gets consumed; the deepest moat is an ecosystem.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### power
**What it answers:** which posture does the brand project, and is it confused?
**Use it when** signals feel mixed, or the company has grown out of its old voice.
**Give it** the brand, its market condition and rivals.
**You get** the figure the brand executes against the one it intends, a scan for contradictory signals across touchpoints, the best combination of postures, and a map of when to shift posture as the market moves.
**How it reasons:** read the market condition first, then choose the gesture; the danger is confused power more than weak power.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### rhyming
**What it answers:** what should we launch next so the portfolio compounds?
**Use it when** planning the next product, service or line.
**Give it** your launch history and the candidate moves.
**You get** your launches scored as music on a five-line staff of value stages, the movement, beats and tempo, the patterns and antipatterns found, a recommended next move with alternatives, each anchored to a well-known precedent, and the map of what to add, substitute or remove at each horizon.
**How it reasons:** winners extend, losers orphan; rhyme with your own patterns and land in time with the market.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### bmc
**What it answers:** what does the business model look like, per audience and per horizon?
**Use it** at the start as a reference, and at the end as a deliverable.
**Give it** the upstream work.
**You get** business model canvases per audience and per horizon, showing which blocks change when a trigger fires, with every block traced to its source or flagged as an assumption.
**How it reasons:** the model differs by audience and evolves across horizons; one static canvas hides both.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy* (the canvas is by Osterwalder and Pigneur).

## Take it to market

### tactics
**What it answers:** where do we land first, and how do we enter without a frontal fight?
**Use it when** planning a launch or a move against incumbents.
**Give it** your position, your pursuers and your objective.
**You get** the priority, the category conditioned before the launch, the beachhead with the signal that it is held, the moves to make, three preparatory strikes (condition, prove, mobilize) into the big event, the first launch content, and the outline of the three horizons.
**How it reasons:** the market is wilderness; a fight is the most expensive way to gain ground, and a big event lands only on prepared ground.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy* and *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### workplan
**What it answers:** who does what, by when, with which budget?
**Use it when** the strategy is decided and needs to become work.
**Give it** the strategy, the team, the budget and the dates.
**You get** workstreams with deliverables, owners, dates, dependencies and budget, milestones locked to the strikes, a RACI, a capacity check with the cuts it implies, the critical path, the risks and the review rhythm.
**How it reasons:** every action cites the decision it executes; an action that executes nothing is cut.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### social
**What it answers:** what do we publish, where and when, and how do we run the community?
**Use it when** building or repairing social and content around a strategy.
**Give it** the strategy, your accounts and your team.
**You get** platforms chosen for the value they carry, content pillars drawn from your values, an editorial calendar locked to the strikes, a community playbook (including how to turn away customers your premise excludes), KPIs per pillar and sample posts.
**How it reasons:** a platform that carries no value for a named audience is noise to maintain.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### events
**What it answers:** which events carry each strike, and how do we run them?
**Use it when** planning a launch sequence or the big event.
**Give it** the strikes, the audiences and the budget.
**You get** one brief per event: the format matched to the strike, the single message, the proof shown, audience and invitations, date and run of show, content before and after, logistics, owner, and the signal that unlocks the next strike.
**How it reasons:** each strike prepares the next; the confirming signal decides when to advance.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### horizon-playbooks
**What it answers:** what do we launch at each horizon, and what condition fires it?
**Use it when** you want growth to happen on evidence instead of on a calendar.
**Give it** the scenarios, the rhyming read and the triggers.
**You get** a playbook per horizon: entry conditions with thresholds, the move on the portfolio and what it rhymes with, the play and its budget reserve, what to prepare in advance, a readiness state (dormant, prepare, armed, fire) and abort conditions.
**How it reasons:** a play has to be ready before its trigger fires, because the window it opens is short.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### engineering
**What it answers:** which KPIs, tests and automations keep it measurable?
**Use it** before a launch, or when the dashboard has stopped meaning anything.
**Give it** the plan and your tools.
**You get** channel briefs, proof specifications, a short KPI plan with a guard against metric decay, a validation plan with a technique per risky assumption (landing page, fake door, pre-sale, concierge, Wizard of Oz, lateral tests), the automation for launch content, a trigger monitor and an onboarding kit.
**How it reasons:** metrics multiply and decay; a few renewed indicators tied to the promise beat a dashboard nobody reads.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

## Keep it current

### change-scan
**What it answers:** what changed, and which part of the proposition does it hit?
**Use it when** the market, the strategy or the team has moved.
**Give it** what you know has changed; it adds what the sources show.
**You get** a register of internal and external changes, each with evidence, date, direction and strength, mapped to the factor of the Formula and the part of the 3P it weakens.
**How it reasons:** a change matters by what it does to the proposition, whatever its size in the news.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### drift-test
**What it answers:** how far has our proposition drifted from today's market?
**Use it when** the proposition feels tired but nobody can say why.
**Give it** the current proposition and the change register.
**You get** the fifteen challenges rescored with what changed color and why, the values turning into standards, the rungs no longer earned, the posture out of step, and the proofs that stopped convincing.
**How it reasons:** a stable value is no longer valuable; the reserve pool has to field a successor before the old value expires.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### scenarios
**What it answers:** what could plausibly happen next, and how do we respond to each case?
**Use it when** planning under uncertainty, or before committing to a direction.
**Give it** the drivers you see; it adds trends and rivals' moves.
**You get** three or four plausible scenarios built on the most uncertain, highest-impact drivers, each with a narrative, the rival moves it implies, signposts with thresholds and dates, its effect on the proposition, a pre-decided response, and the moves that are right in every scenario.
**How it reasons:** scenarios are argued for plausibility and refused when they are only wishes; their value lies in the responses decided before they happen.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Strategy*.

### redline
**What it answers:** what do we keep, adjust or replace, and in which order?
**Use it when** evolving an existing proposition.
**Give it** the change scan, the drift test and the scenarios.
**You get** every element of the proposition marked keep, adjust or replace, with the change that justifies it, before and after side by side, and the order: what to change now and what waits for its trigger.
**How it reasons:** only what the evidence touches changes; the rest is carried forward and said to be unchanged.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

## Check it against reality

### progress-review
**What it answers:** what was done, not done, delayed, or done and not working?
**Use it** at each review date, after a strike, or when the plan and reality drift apart.
**Give it** the plan and where the evidence lives.
**You get** a status for every planned item with its evidence, delays with their new dates, and the unplanned work with its reason.
**How it reasons:** settle the facts before discussing causes, and keep apart what was delivered without result (a question for the strategy) from what was never delivered (a question for the plan).
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### kpi-readout
**What it answers:** what do the numbers say against the targets and the triggers?
**Use it** alongside the progress review.
**Give it** your analytics, CRM and reports.
**You get** each KPI against target and baseline, each validation test passed, failed or inconclusive, the triggers fired or close, and the metrics that stopped tracking anything.
**How it reasons:** each number is read with its baseline, period and sample size before it counts as evidence.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### root-cause
**What it answers:** why did each gap open?
**Use it when** something was delayed or did not work.
**Give it** the progress review.
**You get** a cause for each gap, classed as execution, plan, strategy, market or measurement, with its evidence, and repeated causes grouped into one problem.
**How it reasons:** the fix has to land at the level of the cause; a strategy problem patched with more execution stays a strategy problem.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

### realign
**What it answers:** what changes in the plan, the strategy and the documents?
**Use it** at the end of a review.
**Give it** the review, the readout and the causes.
**You get** a decision per item (continue, fix, re-plan, drop, escalate), strategy changes limited to the assumptions the evidence disproved, a rebuilt plan, the list of document sections to update, lessons, and the next review date.
**How it reasons:** escalate only what failed; everything else stays, and the documents follow reality.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Engineering*.

## Package it

### guidelines
**What it answers:** how does the team apply the proposition consistently?
**Use it when** the people who execute need rules and examples.
**You get** values and antithesis, premise and the courteous farewell, promise wording and rung, the proof always to show, the posture, tone of voice, brand personality, mission, vision and purpose, and do and do-not phrasing.
**Read more in** *Value Proposition Design*.

### deck
**What it answers:** how do we present the strategy?
**Use it** for a board, a team or investors.
**You get** the argument on slides, one idea per slide, with speaker notes and the open decisions flagged.

### cheatsheet
**What it answers:** what does the team need on one page?
**Use it** for onboarding or as a desk reference.
**You get** a one-page summary of the rules, steps, tests and failure modes for your project.

### independent-check
**What it answers:** does this document hold up when someone else scores it?
**Use it** before anything ships; every job runs it automatically.
**You get** a fresh score from the document alone, with the evidence for each criterion and any gap against the original self-score.

# Humanizing the prose - remove AI writing tells

Every document, slide, spreadsheet caption, and reasoning file this method produces must read as if a person wrote it for one reader, not as generic machine prose. Before any deliverable is finalized, pass its prose through this check and edit the tells out. Keep every fact, number, name, date, and claim exactly: never invent one to smooth a sentence, and never drop one to remove a tell. This changes how the text reads, not what it says.

## The principle
A model writes the most likely next words, which fit the widest range of readers, so its prose stages importance, falls into rhythm by rule, inflates ordinary facts, decorates formatting, and leaves chat residue. A person writes for one reader and one subject, so their choices are specific and uneven. Remove the default choices and keep the specific ones.

## The process
1. Mark the tells, strongest first, at sentence and at paragraph scale.
2. Rewrite: state each point plainly, vary sentence length, keep every supported claim.
3. Check: read it aloud, confirm no fact was added or lost, and hunt the five that survive most rewrites: a not-X-but-Y contrast, a one-line closer, a dash, a triad, a bold label.
4. Finalize: if a sentence stays awkward, rewrite the paragraph around its point.

## The tells to remove
Staging instead of stating: "not X but Y" contrasts; one-line closers that restate the paragraph; aphorisms ("at its core", "the real question is", "X is the Y of Z"); staged run-ups ("let's dive in", "here is what you need to know"); arguing with no one ("this isn't about", "to be clear", "a tempting approach would be").

Rhythm by rule: forced triads; several sentences opening the same way; dashes as the universal connector (use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses, and no em or en dashes); stacked qualifiers; hyphenated pairs in every position; needless passive voice.

Inflation and borrowed authority: overused machine words (delve, robust as filler, crucial, pivotal, testament, underscore, landscape as an abstract noun, showcase, tapestry, leverage as filler, and the like); inflated significance ("marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament", "the future looks bright"); vague connection ("associated with"); shallow -ing riders ("highlighting", "underscoring"); sales language ("nestled in the heart of", "vibrant", "breathtaking", "must-visit"); unnamed experts and prestige lists; avoiding is, are and has ("serves as", "boasts", "functions as").

Formatting by rule: bold used as decoration, and every list item given a bold label and a colon; title-case headings, emoji or arrows as decoration, a rule between every section; curly quotes where the format uses straight ones.

Leftovers: chatbot residue ("great question", "I hope this helps", "let me know"); knowledge-limit disclaimers and plausible guesses; a heading restated in its first sentence; writing about the previous version outside a changelog.

## When not to act
A person may make any one of these on purpose, so the weaker tells (a single dash, one qualifier, passive voice, hyphenated pairs, curly quotes) count only when several appear together in the same passage. Never strip a real business specific: a number, a named competitor, a date, a sourced figure, a proof point, a first-person judgement the author can defend, or a genuine caveat, scope statement, or legal note. Removing tells is half the job; the result must still sound like a person and still carry every fact.

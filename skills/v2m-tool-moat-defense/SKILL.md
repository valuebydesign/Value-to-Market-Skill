# Moat and Defense

Uniqueness made structural is the construct that gives you time. A castle without a moat is a beautiful target. But the defensibility ladder is not the moat alone: **Leverage** (a force you apply), **Bridge** (an alliance you build), and **Moat** (a structure you hold) are three constructs, and a defense that names only the moat leaves two thirds of the board unplayed.

## On invocation
Read `references/output-protocol.md`, `references/frameworks.md` (Leverage/Bridge/Moat, Defensibility Ladder, Assets), `references/scorecards.md`. Pull the asset sweep; ask the likely attackers, and the suppliers and customers whose behaviour you would want to shift.

## Procedure
1. Classify assets on the Defensibility Ladder in benefit terms.
2. **Moat - what you hold.** The brutal question: what would a rival with ten times your capital still fail to replicate? If nothing, say so. Name the moat type(s) across the seven; state each one's classic attack and your exposure. Maintenance and resentment: welcomed vs resentment moats. Internal-drain flag: where the purchasing department, not the enemy, drains it.
3. **Bridge - what you build, with killer features.** A bridge is an alliance or dependency that ties a supplier or a customer to you. For each key supplier and customer relationship, propose the **killer feature** that builds the bridge: the specific capability, integration, or offer that makes them want to connect and costly to disconnect (an integration that becomes their workflow, an ingredient-brand tie, exclusive data or supply, a co-built feature). Name who it binds, why they cross, and what the bridge protects.
4. **Leverage - the force you apply, not only the asset.** Leverage is any force that shifts the equilibrium, not just a held asset. For each, name the target (supplier, customer, rival, ecosystem), the mechanism, and its register:
   - **Nudge** - a light force that makes the desired behaviour the easy default (defaults, incentives, framing, small switching aids).
   - **Convince** - evidence, proof, or narrative that changes what the other party believes and wants.
   - **Force** - a hard lever that constrains the other party's options (a standard you set, a dependency, exclusivity, a regulatory or contractual hook).
   State how each leverage changes the force equilibrium and at what cost or exposure.
5. **Equilibrium read.** Put moat, bridges and leverages together: where the balance of power sits today, and which one or two moves would tilt it in your favour.

## Recommendation
The full defense set: the moat to build on (or the honest "nothing yet" and how to earn one) with its maintenance cost; the **killer features to build bridges** to named suppliers and customers; and the **leverages** (nudge / convince / force) to apply to shift the equilibrium, each with its target and cost. Score against the Moat scorecard.

Follow `output-protocol.md` for the reasoning file, self-score, and next step. Always produce the branded Word report, and since this tool is list-heavy always generate a branded Excel: a moat tab (moat type, what survives the ten-times test, attack vector, maintenance cost), a bridge tab (relationship, supplier or customer, killer feature, why they cross, what it protects), and a leverage tab (target, mechanism, register nudge/convince/force, equilibrium effect, cost or exposure).
